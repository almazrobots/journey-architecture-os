# Employee research ethics

Employee journey research has one feature customer research usually lacks: the organization running the study employs the participants and decides their pay, progression, and continued employment. Every rule below follows from that power asymmetry. These rules are practice guidance, not legal advice; the checks marked "jurisdiction-dependent" must be answered by the organization's legal, privacy, and employee-relations functions before fieldwork.

General research ethics (consent, minimization, retention, pseudonymization, recordings, AI tools on transcripts) apply in full. If the `journey-research` skill is installed, follow its research-ethics reference as the baseline; this file adds what is specific to employees.

## Power asymmetry

- Employees may feel unable to decline, may say what they think the employer wants to hear, and may fear that criticism will be traced back to them. Design for this; do not assume candor.
- Researchers should not sit in the participant's reporting line. Where possible, use a researcher from another function or an external researcher for sensitive topics (performance, leave, conflict, exits).
- Say explicitly what the research will not be used for: individual performance assessment, disciplinary decisions, selection for redundancy, or monitoring.

## Voluntariness

- Invitations come from the research team, not from the participant's manager. Managers may announce that a study exists; they do not nominate, chase, or learn who took part.
- Declining has no consequence, and no one records who declined.
- Sessions take place in working time without loss of pay, or are compensated the same way for everyone.
- Offer a channel that does not require being seen (anonymous diary entry, written response) for people who will not speak in a room.

## Anonymity and reporting thresholds

- Report no result for a group smaller than a minimum size; five is a common floor, and many organizations use a higher one for surveys. Apply the threshold to every cut: team, location, tenure band, demographic group, and their combinations.
- Watch for re-identification by subtraction: if the total and all but one subgroup are reported, the last subgroup is exposed.
- Quotes from small teams identify people through vocabulary, role, and events. Generalize or omit.
- Do not promise anonymity you cannot guarantee. "Confidential" (the team knows who said what but will not disclose it) and "anonymous" (no one can link data to a person) are different promises; use the one that is true.

## Manager and leadership access to raw data

- Managers, HR business partners, and leaders receive findings at or above the reporting threshold, never raw recordings, transcripts, or session-level notes.
- Access to raw material is limited to named researchers and recorded.
- Requests to "just see what my team said" are declined by default; the answer is part of the study's published rules.
- Findings about a named manager or small team go first to a senior sponsor agreed in advance, framed as a service or system issue, not a verdict on a person.

## Retaliation risk

- Assess before fieldwork: which topics could expose participants to retaliation (reporting misconduct, criticizing a manager, disclosing health or caring needs)?
- Route disclosures of misconduct, safety issues, or harassment to the organization's established channels, with the participant's agreement, and keep them out of the journey findings.
- After reporting, watch for signs of retaliation in the studied group (sudden performance actions, reassignment) and have a named contact participants can reach.

## Employee data from systems

Journey work often uses HR, IT, and workplace system data: ticket histories, access logs, badge data, collaboration-tool metadata.

- Using such data to study individuals' behavior can amount to employee monitoring. Aggregate first; prefer counts and durations per cohort over per-person traces.
- Jurisdiction-dependent: whether analysis of system data counts as monitoring, which lawful basis applies, whether a data-protection impact assessment is required, and whether employees must be notified. The UK ICO's guidance on monitoring workers is one example of how a regulator frames these questions.
- Jurisdiction-dependent: whether employee representatives must be informed, consulted, or must agree. In some countries a works council has co-determination rights over technical systems capable of monitoring behavior or performance (for example Germany, Works Constitution Act § 87(1) no. 6); collective agreements may add rules, and the GDPR (Article 88) allows member states to set specific rules for employment data.
- Do not repurpose operational data collected for another purpose without checking whether the new use is compatible and notified.

## Pre-fieldwork checklist

- [ ] Stated non-uses (performance, discipline, redundancy, monitoring) written into the study notice.
- [ ] Researchers outside participants' reporting lines for sensitive topics.
- [ ] Invitations and scheduling run by the research team; managers do not know who took part.
- [ ] Minimum reporting group size set and applied to all cuts.
- [ ] Raw-data access limited to named researchers; manager access rule published.
- [ ] Retaliation risk assessed; disclosure routes agreed.
- [ ] Legal/privacy: lawful basis, notice, DPIA need, monitoring rules confirmed.
- [ ] Employee representatives: information, consultation, or agreement requirements confirmed where they exist.
